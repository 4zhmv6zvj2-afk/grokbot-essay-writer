# Academic writing helpers

**Status:** Router only. Not a faculty style sheet. Not a build plan. A reflective diary is a third kind of writing, in its own helper. This note does not copy that helper. Pasted into research and write tool text, so this note does not name a shell.

## What this note is

When the brief does not name a module-specific writing guide (LD handbook, faculty style sheet, or equivalent in the brief), the writer uses the academic skills notes as **shape and process helpers**. These helpers never override:

1. The **module brief** for that essay id (task, word count, sections, voice, marking criteria).
2. **Do not invent sources** and **approve the save** (README and `docs/workflow.md`).
3. **Harvard Referencing** for citations and the reference list (`docs/referencing-harvard.md`). Academic skills pages may mention Harvard in a generic sense; this tool stays on Harvard Referencing, not APA.

If a later note supplies a module writing guide, that guide **replaces** these helpers for writing shape on that module. Referencing stays Harvard Referencing unless the brief explicitly names another style (none do today).

These notes are shape only, and only when the brief names no writing guide for that module. Synthetic teaching patterns inform briefs and rubrics only, not student writing as a model (`docs/samples-study.md`). Slack stays out of this tool. The draft is model-assisted; the student owns what they submit and must follow their school's rules. No student name or number unless the brief requires one.

The ten stage ids stay one path. The brief picks the helper file. There is no second stage list and no second draft stage. Chat and status use the label for that brief. This router names both labels where the name differs.

## After research, revisit the plan

After the annotated bibliography and the critical review are saved, revisit the stage 3 plan before the first draft. Revisit stage 2 only if the research breaks that lock. On an essay the lock is the thesis. On a report the lock is purpose and scope. On a diary the lock is the entries the brief names. The report’s one-sentence answer is the central theme. A thin edit of that sentence is allowed. A break is not a thin edit. A report has no thesis. A diary has no thesis.

Check that the section arguments still hold, that the word weights still match the brief, and that each source type is a real title, with gaps named. The one-sentence answer should still be the best one. Leave out anything the research never showed. Do not invent sources. Use only the year printed in the opened source. Do not add a second year.

If anything changes, show what changed and why, from the gathered sources, and record that in the tracking note. Approve that save again before the first draft. If the thesis breaks, or if purpose and scope or the central theme breaks, approve stage 2 again and then approve stage 3 again. Do not silently rewrite the plan into the draft.

## Stage checklists

### 1. Analyse the task

Understand the question and **marking guideline**: **task words**, **content words**, and **limiting words** in the brief for this essay id.

**Guideline:** Academic skills, Answering assignment & essay questions, plus the module brief and any rubric or marking criteria in `docs/samples-study.md`.

**Basics checklist:**
- Respond to the question. Split the brief into task words, content words, and limiting words.
- The task word sets the shape of the answer. Use the clue-word list in docs/academic-writing-helpers.md. Discuss is for and against, then a conclusion. Compare is similarities and differences. Contrast is differences. Evaluate is a supported judgement with advantages and disadvantages. Do not treat clue words as the same job.
- Content words set the topic. Limiting words narrow it. The required length sets how much research and how deep.
- Note assessment rules and formatting from the course outline. The module brief still wins.
- Genre-from-brief: essay, report, or a reflective diary of entries (MOD1004AA = report). A reflective section inside an essay or a report stays that kind of writing. Do not default to essay shape.
- Marking guideline or rubric noted before you approve later saves.
- Listed mark-scheme sections and the brief's own word-count or section-length rules win over these helpers and sample notes. Do not invent a word budget or per-section count the brief and rubric do not state.
- If the brief names a database, keep that name and a slot for a file the student holds. Do not look that database up, and do not store a folder path.

### 2. Essay: Establish a thesis. Report: Establish purpose and scope. Diary: Establish the entries

Read the helper the brief picked. Essay: lock the argument in one or two sentences (`docs/essay-writing-helpers.md`). Report: lock purpose and scope (`docs/report-writing-helpers.md`). Diary: lock the entries the brief names (`docs/reflective-diary-helpers.md`). A report has no thesis. A diary has no thesis. Chat shows one label, the brief’s. Approve the save before the lock.

**Guideline:** Academic skills, How to write a university essay, for an essay thesis. Business School *Writing a report* for a report’s purpose and scope.

**Basics checklist:**
- Record an initial response, possible answers, opinions, and what you already know, including related topics or frameworks, before the lock. An essay records this before the thesis is locked. A report records this before purpose and scope are locked.
- Question the terms. Is there an agreed approach? If not, how the approaches differ, which one you will use, and how the key concepts relate. On a report, those terms apply to purpose and scope.
- Broaden first (brainstorm, keep the ideas), then narrow. On an essay, the locked thesis is the narrowed claim. On a report, the locked purpose and scope is the narrowed claim.
- An essay thesis answers the question in one or two sentences you can discuss throughout. A report locks purpose and scope.
- The essay is balanced. Opposing views are examined only when a gathered source states them.
- Argument direction clear before you approve this save. Genre-from-brief still respected.

### 3. Essay: Write an essay plan. Report: Write a report plan. Diary: Write a diary plan

Read the helper the brief picked. An essay plan is introduction, body, and conclusion. A report plan is the brief’s headed sections. A diary plan is one part for each entry the brief names. Default report section jobs, the executive summary, and headings are the Business School *Writing a report* PDF in `docs/report-writing-helpers.md`. The engineering case-study body is not the default plan. Approve the save before the plan is locked.

**Guideline:** the helper the brief picked.

**Basics checklist:**
- An essay plan holds the thesis and the main points as the reasons for that answer, in logical order. A report plan holds the central theme and the headed sections that develop it.
- Scale the number of points to the length. An essay of about 1,000 to 1,500 words aims for 3 to 4 main points. A longer report scales its points to that length and to its sections.
- An essay plan outlines the introduction, the body, and the conclusion. A report plan uses the brief’s sections and the report section jobs in docs/academic-writing-helpers.md.
- Each main point has room for reasoning and gathered evidence, and names sources you expect to use.
- Revise the plan after research. If the plan is itself assessed, follow that assessment’s form.
- Genre-from-brief: essay flow versus report sections. Do not default to essay shape.
- Section headings and word weights in the plan follow only what the brief, rubric, or mark scheme lists. Do not invent a section list or word budget.
- When the student hands in an outline or their own prose, that outline is the plan. Keep its headings and their order, and do not add a section the outline does not name.

### 4. Research the topic

**4a — Annotated bibliography.** First pass: gathered sources only. Each note is a few full sentences: what the paper did, what it found, and how it helps this assignment. **Approve the save** when the bibliography is ready to lock.

**Guideline:** Academic skills, Annotated bibliography. When written up, citations use **Harvard Referencing**, not APA.

**Basics checklist (4a):**
- Gathered sources only (do not invent sources). Choose sources that answer the question, cover more than one view, and include the key works.
- Each annotation is a few full sentences after the citation, written so a student can read them aloud: what the paper did, what it found, and how it helps this assignment. Keep mark-scheme words the brief uses, and gloss each one in plain English the first time. Do not delete those words.
- Evidence vs claim: what the source shows versus what you will argue.
- Harvard Referencing 4a format: the citation line stays exact, then a few full sentences a student can read aloud. Those sentences say what the paper did, what it found, and how it helps this assignment. Keep mark-scheme words the brief uses, and gloss each one in plain English the first time. Do not delete those words. Do not replace that paragraph with a shortened bibliography or a citation line that has no sentences. Journal: Surname, Initial. (Year) 'Article title', Journal Title, volume(issue), pp. start-end. Available at: doi:10.xxxx/xxxxx. Book: Surname, Initial. (Year) Title. Place: Publisher. Web page: Organisation (Year) Title. Available at: URL (Accessed: date). A missing page, DOI, volume, journal, place, publisher, or access date stays blank. Do not invent it. The full reference pass is stage 9.
- download-list.docx is leftovers only: gathered sources not already in the papers zip, plus failed look-up addresses that are not saved PDFs. Do not repeat citation lines from annotation.docx.
- When you name other gathered sources or free alternatives already found after this save, speech only. Do not open a new look-up or free-alt download in this step.
- After this save is approved, a later download step can fetch leftover free alternatives already found. That step does not open a new look-up and does not invent an address.
- The citation year is the year in the PDF page title or header. Do not use the file's modified time as the year.
- An abstract-only source stays on download-list.docx until a PDF of that source is opened. It is not an annotated-bibliography entry before that PDF is opened.
- A PDF larger than 20MB is read in parts on this machine. Each part stays readable. A part that cannot be read is recorded for the later citation pass. The PDF bytes stay on this machine.
- Before a Word file or a zip is handed across, clear the Mac quarantine mark and the provenance mark on the Documents copy. If those marks are absent, leave the file unchanged.
- A database the brief names is not a look-up. The file the student holds fills that slot. Do not store a folder path.
- Research file work on this computer opens only a path already allowed for this sequence. Allowed: the code checkout, and that checkout is code only, not a student save; Essay-writer/<module>/ workflow files; uploads/<module>/ when that is the product path; a path already named on the download list, the annotation, or a known project file for this sequence; local subject notes for this module when the module needs it. **4b — Critical review.** Deep read of selected papers (aim, methods, scope, evaluation, fit to the argument). Output is one Word file per paper in `critical-reviews.zip`. **Approve the save** when that review is ready to lock.

**Guideline:** Academic skills, Critical review.

**Basics checklist (4b):**

- Research file work on this computer opens only a path already allowed for this sequence. Allowed: the code checkout, and that checkout is code only, not a student save; Essay-writer/<module>/ workflow files; uploads/<module>/ when that is the product path; a path already named on the download list, the annotation, or a known project file for this sequence; local subject notes for this module when the module needs it. - A critical review judges the text. It is not a negative attack. Read the content, the purpose, the audience, and how the text is built.
- Record the aim, the methods, the scope, what the text adds, what it leaves out, and whether the conclusions follow from the evidence.
- Evidence vs claim. Bias, validity, and reliability only from the gathered text. Opposing views only if a gathered source states them. Do not invent them.
- Fit to the argument on the papers you will use.

### 5. Write your first draft

The name is the same for both genres. Essay shape is in `docs/essay-writing-helpers.md`. Report section jobs default to the Business School *Writing a report* PDF in `docs/report-writing-helpers.md`. The engineering case-study body is not that default. There is no second draft stage.

**Guideline:** the helper the brief picked. Word is draft, then approve the save (`docs/workflow.md`).

**Basics checklist:**
- Write the report or the essay as an argument that answers the question. The critical-review notes are evidence for that argument. Do not paste those notes in as the draft.
- The draft shows the framework, how you will answer, and which gathered evidence and examples you will use. It is not the final essay.
- Essay introduction follows this argument: a thesis that answers the question, and a road map of the main ideas. Do not open with a general sentence built only from the topic nouns.
- The body answers by developing the discussion. It shows grasp of what you read, with exposition, evidence, and relevant examples.
- If the question has more than one part, give each part its own stretch of the body.
- Each body paragraph is one idea: a topic sentence, supporting sentences, evidence or an example, and analysis of why that evidence matters. A paragraph closer must not only restate the topic sentence. Make it assignment-specific: a cited finding, a limit, or a figure.
- Essay conclusion stays with this argument and adds no new information. Do not close with an automatic In conclusion that only restates the topic. End on an assignment-specific cited finding, a limit, or a figure.
- Paraphrase, summary, and quotation follow the source-use note in docs/academic-writing-helpers.md.
- In-text citations are Harvard Referencing: family name and year. The first Word reference list uses each gathered source in full (author, year, title, and publisher or URL when that source has them). Do not leave a surname alone as the entry.
- Report briefs use the report section jobs in docs/academic-writing-helpers.md, not this essay shape. A reflection section uses the reflective-writing note. A case analysis uses the case-analysis note.
- Opposing views only from gathered material. Do not invent sources. Approve the draft before treating it as locked for Word.
- Do not set the conclusion label to noted or open on the Word cover. The person chooses that on validate before Word.
- Do not write an independent re-review or mark your own essay passed. Only the separate reviewer records a pass.
- The entire first-draft stage is AI-authored. The person does not read or edit Word in this stage.
- On first use in student-facing prose, gloss jargon and acronyms in plain English (for example GHG Scope 1, 2, and 3). The example is not a closed list.
- Plain-English gloss only. Do not invent a company's methods or data practices. Name a method only when an opened gathered file states it in a sentence you can point to.
- Chat for this step stays quiet. Do not paste this checklist into the chat, and do not name a file path. The person sees the finished first draft after the save. A middle pass stays quiet unless the draft is blocked.
- When the critical-review notes are too long for this step, drop only a repeated model line, then take whole notes in groups that fit, then re-open a source only for a gap that note does not cover. Do not cut off the end of a note without saying so. Do not invent a quotation that is not in the full note. Keep going while a group or a re-opened passage still fits. Stop and tell the person only when none of those ways fits.
- When the student supplied an outline or base prose, its headings and their order stay. Add no section and no body text around whatever was gathered, and weave a short cite only where a gathered paper supports the student's own sentence.
- Keep the commitment the writer means. Where they are unsure, keep a modal or an epistemic marker they would say. Where they endorse or resist a source, use a reporting verb that matches. Do not flatten a hedged claim into a bare assertion, and do not add a hedge they did not mean.
- Tie each claim to one thing the writer can point at: a passage, a figure, a date, a result, or a scene in the course text. If the sentence could open any essay on the topic, it is still a summary. Replace it with the specific thing this assignment is about.
- Revise for what a reader still cannot see: cut, reorder, and add. If a chatbot produced the paragraph, rewrite the ideas in the writer's sentences. Change the words, reorder, delete, and add. Do not polish the model's sentences in place.
- Every source sentence comes from a source the writer opened. A paraphrase keeps that source's specific claim. Delete a citation or a paraphrase they cannot find in the opened file. Do not list a writing tool as an author.
- Write for the brief and the handbook. No planted errors, no forced slang, no forced first person, and no synonym spinning. Do not chase a third-party similarity or AI-score number; that number is not the target.
- A claim is no stronger than the evidence under it. One case does not show a general trend, a correlation does not show a cause, and a small sample does not show a wide effect. Keep the limit the source states, and if it states none, write the claim at the size the evidence supports.
- When the sources you opened do not settle a point, say that the evidence is mixed or thin. Do not fill the gap with an assertion, and do not present one source as the settled view. A gap is part of the answer, not a line to hide.
- When the brief or the mark scheme lists marking criteria, each part of the draft serves one of them. Use the criteria it lists and no others: do not add a criterion, a weighting, or a band the brief does not print. A point that serves no listed criterion still has to answer the question.
- Keep the person the brief names. A diary keeps that brief voice and is not forced into third person. An essay or a report is not forced into first person to sound human. Ban AI-glue: do not pad with stock transitions, empty emphasis, or lines that could sit on any assignment. When no lecture, reading, or workplace detail is on the opened files, ask the student once for one real detail and use only that detail. Never invent a lecture, a reading, or a workplace detail. Do not open with a general sentence built only from the topic nouns. Do not close with an automatic In conclusion that only restates the topic. A paragraph closer must not only restate the topic sentence. Make it assignment-specific: a cited finding, a limit, or a figure. Use a connective only when the relation is true. However has to mark a real contrast. More connectives are not more coherence.
- Vary the sentence rhythm. Cut AI connectors such as Furthermore, Moreover, In conclusion, and It is important to note. Do not open a diary with a polished line such as In this reflective diary I will. A diary may be slightly fragmented. An essay or a report body stays academic and uses Harvard Referencing. Prefer a concrete detail over an abstract line. Do not plant grammar errors. Use words the writer can define out loud. Do not nominalize a verb into -ion, -ment, or -ance to sound scientific, and do not swap in a rarer synonym the writer does not use.
- Cut words that do no work in the sentence. Empty intensifiers such as very, really, quite, and extremely add nothing, and significantly adds nothing when no figure follows it. Filler phrases such as in order to, due to the fact that, and it is worth noting that have a short plain form: to, because, and nothing. Keep at most one hedge in a sentence. A stack such as it could perhaps be argued that or it may possibly be the case that is not more careful, it is only less clear. A single hedge stays only when the writer means it.
- Do not write as an assistant or as a model. No note that the writer is an AI, no apology for a model limit, and no offer to keep helping. The essay speaks as the student, not as a tool.
- Do not start more than one paragraph with the same words. An opener such as In today's world, Throughout history, or This essay will discuss could sit on any assignment, so cut it and start on the claim. Repeating a key term is not the same thing.
- Use a section lean, an example, or a surprise only when the student gave that detail after the plan was saved. A skip or silence leaves that slot empty. Do not invent that detail.

### 7. Ask for feedback

Read the stage 6 draft, ask questions, then edit from those answers. A light check covers currency and academic tone and clarity. Heavy file work is a separate file step. The later edit, the references, and the final Word each run on their own, and only after those questions and edits are saved.

**Guideline:** Module brief and rubric; Academic skills, Editing your writing, for how to use feedback.

**Basics checklist:**
- Read the stage 6 draft, ask questions, then edit from those answers.
- Ask about structure, argument, and clarity before any later step. Feedback is tied to the brief or rubric. It is not evidence.
- A light check covers currency and academic tone and clarity. It does not open a new look-up.

## Using sources in the sentence

From Academic skills, Grammar and writing essentials. The citation format is still Harvard Referencing.

| Move | What you write |
| --- | --- |
| Paraphrase | The source’s meaning in your words, close to the original detail. Cite it. A particular point also takes the page. |
| Summary | Much shorter. Core ideas only. Leave out the source’s examples. Cite it. A summary of a whole work does not need a page. |
| Quotation | The author’s words, in quotation marks, with the page. A short quote stays in the paragraph. A long quote is its own indented paragraph and does not need quotation marks. An omission is an ellipsis. Words you add are in square brackets. Do not change the meaning. |

A paraphrase keeps that source's specific claim. It does not replace the finding with a vaguer summary of the topic. Every source sentence comes from a source the writer opened.

Bring a source in for a reason: it carries authority, it is a position you will discuss, it is evidence for your point, or you need to separate one author from another or from yourself. Say what the author does, and whether you agree, disagree, or are reporting: states, argues, suggests, concedes, concludes.

Vary the transitions by the job they do: sequence, example, emphasis, time, comparison, contrast, cause, addition, clarification, summary. Do not lean on one linking word.

## Academic style

From business-school *Writing in academic style*. Academic style is the usual choice: you are writing for the lecturer. Use another style only when the brief asks for a client, a public reader, or a professional voice.

The writing shows that you understand the topic, can take a position, can argue for it, and can say why the argument matters. The organisation is visible from the introduction. This is the finished line of thought, not a transcript of you working it out, and not a textbook talking down to a beginner.

- Third person is the usual voice. First person is rare. Second person is not used. A reflective section is the exception when the brief allows it.
- No contractions. Prefer a single verb to a phrasal verb. No colloquial wording and no language aimed at an emotional effect.
- When a generalisation needs a hedge the writer means, a marker such as probably, in most cases, seems, or might can stay.
- Hedge a generalisation only when the writer means that hedge. Keep the modal, the epistemic marker, or the reporting verb they would say. Do not flatten a hedged claim, and do not add a hedge they did not mean.
- Use words the writer can define out loud. Do not nominalize a verb into -ion, -ment, or -ance to sound scientific, and do not swap in a rarer synonym.
- Order the paragraph as this argument. Do not open with a general sentence built only from the topic nouns. A paragraph closer must not only restate the topic sentence. Make it assignment-specific: a cited finding, a limit, or a figure. Do not close with an automatic In conclusion that only restates the topic.
- Use a connective only when the relation is true. More connectives are not more coherence.
- If a chatbot produced a paragraph, rewrite the ideas in the writer's sentences. Do not polish the model's sentences in place. Write for the brief and the handbook. No planted errors, no forced slang, no forced first person, and no synonym spinning.
- Tie sentences by repeating the key words, or by “this” plus a summary noun. Do not chain “it” and “them” where the reader has to guess the referent.

A report-only voice line is in `docs/report-writing-helpers.md`. That voice is the Business School *Writing a report* PDF: no I, we, or you; an imagined client; clear and direct; respectful and authoritative. It is not taken from the engineering pages.
