# Reflective diary helpers

**Status:** Diary shape only. Not a faculty style sheet. An essay brief does not use this file. A report brief does not use this file.

## Precedence

The mark scheme is the constitution: it says what good is. The brief is the engagement: it says what to do, how long the work is, how it looks, and what the title page carries. Where either one disagrees with this guide, the document wins. That is the whole document, not only the format line.

Where the mark scheme and the brief disagree on what is scored, on the format, on the word band, or on alignment, the mark scheme wins. Where the brief states the task, the title page, identity, and the module code, the brief prevails.

Do not invent sources. Approve the save before a stage is locked. Citations stay Harvard Referencing (`docs/referencing-harvard.md`). A brief that says to spell every author in the text does not replace that guide. In the text, two or three authors are all named. Four or more authors use et al. The reference list names every author.

Shared checklists, clue words, source use, and academic style are in `docs/academic-writing-helpers.md`. This file does not contain a second list of ten stages.

## When to open this file

Open this file when the whole assignment is a reflective diary of entries. Useful signals, read together:

- The brief names the work a reflective diary, a learning diary, or a reflective journal of entries.
- It asks for several entries, such as one entry for each occasion the brief names.
- Each entry is a decision or a learning event, plus analysis, and the writer’s own response is part of the work.

One of those phrases on its own is not enough. The task has to be the diary.

It stays an essay when the brief is an essay. It stays a report when the brief is a report, a case, or a named report module. An essay or a report that contains one reflective section stays that kind of writing. That section keeps the short reflective note in `docs/academic-writing-helpers.md`.

A stray word does not flip the type. Diary instructions sometimes say report for the file, or essay for the prose. The assignment’s name and its task decide.

A logbook, a reflective note, an essay diary, a peer review, or a self-assessment is not this type. If a brief is one of those and it is not a diary of entries, look up how that kind of writing is done, or follow samples the brief itself supplies.

## What reflection is

Two notes sit behind the job. They describe how reflection is written. They do not set the assignment’s headings, word bands, or mark table. The brief and the mark scheme prevail when they conflict with either note.

Academic skills, Reflective writing, is light background. Reflection blends description (what, when, who), analysis (how, why, what if), evaluation (how effective, and why), and a personal voice. It makes meaning from study. It is not a standard essay, not a pure recount, and not a bare right-or-wrong judgement. First person is acceptable when the brief allows it. When the brief is silent, first person is acceptable, and the student can change that before the lock. When the brief forbids it, the brief wins. Other names on that page are a reminder that the forms vary. They are not extra types in this app.

University of Sydney Learning Centre, *Reflective Writing* (2019), is the same kind of background. At university, reflection links practice, a decision, or a learning event to theory. Three levels are enough to know: descriptive, analytical, and critical. A descriptive recount is the foundation and is usually too thin for a marked assignment. Marked reflection is analytical or critical: some distance from the event, another reading of it, a link to theory, and a willingness to question the writer’s own action. As the entry moves from the event to that judgement, the language moves from what was felt to a reasoned opinion.

A mind map is not a required step. Course examples from either note are not stages.

## Diary shape

The entries the brief names, in the brief’s order. Theory is used on a decision or a learning event. There is no single sentence the whole diary has to prove.

The chain inside each entry, adapted to the brief:

1. The decision or the event, with enough context to see what was chosen or what happened, and under what conditions.
2. A theory, model, or gathered source applied to that decision or event. A list of theory names is not this step.
3. The impact the brief asks for. Named goals are in this step only when the brief asks for them. Where the entry claims a benefit, include a downside or a side effect when the material supports one.
4. A real trade-off, limit, or other reading.
5. What the writer learned. When the brief or the top band asks for it, the later entries show what the earlier ones changed.

Drop a move the brief does not ask for. If the brief asks for a recommendation or a next action, that move goes inside the entry. The moves are the contents of an entry. They are not extra headings. The only headings are the headings the brief allows.

## What sample diaries may teach

The chain of moves may teach: a decision against a real alternative, a theory on that decision, the impact the brief asks for, a trade-off or a limit, and what was learned.

Leave these behind: the samples’ length, extra headings the brief does not allow, reference lists, and citation habits. A year printed on a sample is not a source. Do not invent a paper to match one. Do not merge samples into one diary.

## Stages

Stages 4a, 4b, 6, 7, 9, and 10 keep the shared checklists in `docs/academic-writing-helpers.md`. The diary test inside them is below. Stages 2, 3, 5, and 8 are where this file does the work.

### 1. Analyse the task

The chat opens with three things the student can check.

1. How to score high. The top column of the mark table, quoted, and labelled with the words that column actually prints. Lower columns describe weaker work. They are not a second target. If the top column cannot be read, say so and leave the gap empty. Do not invent a band name.
2. What kind of writing this is. A reflective diary, when the signals above match. Do not answer that the brief names no format merely because it never says essay or report.
3. The ask, in the brief’s own words. Restate the aim and the assignment task, including when those headings are not labelled Question or Task.

Also say how many entries, the lengths the brief gives, what the title page carries, and that citations use Harvard Referencing.

Say, briefly, what kind of reflection this is: a personal account that analyses and evaluates, linked to theory, not a feelings log and not a thesis essay. The brief’s own task is the version that is followed.

When the mark table lives only in the brief, the copy used for scoring is `marking-table.md` next to the brief. The student does not receive that copy as part of the diary. The criteria, the marks, the bands, and the words in each cell stay as printed. Do not rename a band. Leave out the marker’s form fields: participant number, comments, strengths, and areas for improvement. If the brief already has its own mark-scheme file, that file stays the copy.

### 2. Establish the entries

An essay locks a thesis. A report locks purpose and scope. A diary locks the entries.

For each entry the brief names, the lock records the decision or the learning event, the other option or the tension if there is one, and why this is the event to write about. It records what the writer did at the time and what they would do with what they now know, when that is part of the brief. It names the line of learning from the first entry to the last when the brief or the top band asks for learning across the entries.

There is no single sentence that the whole diary has to prove.

Record an initial response, then a narrower lock. The lock is still descriptive enough to see what happened, who was involved, and under what conditions. Feeling can be named. The lock is not saved as a feelings diary. The analysis belongs to the plan and the draft.

Approve the save before the lock.

### 3. Write a diary plan

The plan has one part for each entry the brief names, in the brief’s order. Each part uses the chain above, adapted to the brief. Only the headings the brief allows.

Each entry is planned at the length the brief gives. When the brief states a total and a length for each entry or section, both are kept. Title page, references, and appendices sit outside the count unless the brief says otherwise. In-text citations count unless the brief excludes them. If the per-entry lengths and the total cannot be added up, show the clash. Do not silently pick one.

When the brief states only a total, still plan a length for each section the brief names, so the draft is not written as one lump. That split is a plan. It can be changed before it is saved. It is not a rule the brief wrote, and it is not a mark band. Do not invent a tighter band than the brief states.

An entry length stays on the entry. The total stays on the whole body.

Sources are named as support for that entry. The plan is not a literature review.

Approve the save before the plan is locked.

### After research, revisit the plan

After the annotated bibliography and the critical review are saved, revisit the stage 3 plan before the first draft. Revisit the entries only if the research breaks a decision or an event. A thin edit stays on the plan. A break is not a thin edit. A diary has no thesis.

Check that each entry’s chain still holds, that the word weights still match the brief, and that each source type is a real title, with gaps named. Leave out anything the research never showed. Do not invent sources. Use only the year printed in the opened source. Do not add a second year.

If anything changes, show what changed and why, from the gathered sources, and record that in the tracking note. Approve that save again before the first draft. If a decision or an event breaks, approve stage 2 again and then approve stage 3 again. Do not silently rewrite the plan into the draft.

### 4a. Annotated bibliography

Do not restate this stage. Use the checklist in `docs/academic-writing-helpers.md`. Citations follow Harvard Referencing.

A source earns its place because it helps analyse an entry: the decision or the event, the theory, or the impact the brief asks for. The reference list at the end of a sample diary is not a reading list.

### 4b. Critical review

Do not restate this stage. Use the checklist in `docs/academic-writing-helpers.md`. Those notes stay source material. They are not pasted in as the diary entries.

### 5. Write your first draft

The chat stays quiet until the finished draft is saved.

The draft is the entries from the plan, in the voice the brief allows. Each entry walks the chain. Description opens the entry. Analysis applies theory or evidence to the decision or the event. Evaluation judges the impact, including a limit. The writer’s own response says what changed. The wording moves from what was felt at the time to a reasoned judgement, with room for uncertainty and for a concession.

The draft uses only the headings the brief allows. It does not add an essay introduction and conclusion around the entries unless the brief asks for them. It does not add report sections. It does not include the marker’s comments block. It does not copy a sample’s length, its references, or its citation habits.

In-text citations are Harvard Referencing: family name and year. The first Word reference list uses each gathered source in full (author, year, title, and publisher or URL when that source has them). A surname alone is not an entry.

Word counts are met. An entry length stays on the entry, and the total stays on the whole body.

### 6. Re-read and make changes

Do not restate this stage. Use the checklist in `docs/academic-writing-helpers.md`. The read checks the diary: the headings the brief allows, the word counts, the chain in every entry, the voice the brief allows, and learning across the entries when that was asked for. A changed Word file still starts the next copy of the work and leaves the earlier copy in place.

### 7. Ask for feedback

Do not restate this stage. Use the checklist in `docs/academic-writing-helpers.md`. Questions come before any later edit. They are tied to the top band and the brief:

- Is each decision or event explicit, and tied to the occasion the brief names?
- Is a theory or a source applied to it, rather than named and left?
- Is the impact the brief asks for actually judged, including a limit, a cost, or a side effect where one is claimed?
- Does the reasoning hold inside the entry and across the entries?
- Are there only the headings the brief allows?
- Are the word counts inside the brief’s allowance?
- Does the title page carry what the brief asks for, and does identity follow the brief?

The questions are asked again if someone tries to skip them.

### 8. Edit and redraft your diary

The edit improves the diary. It does not turn it into a third-person essay, and it does not add a report conclusion, unless the brief asked for that shape. Cuts remove repetition. They do not turn a true sentence into a false one. Entries that are over or under a per-entry allowance are brought inside it, and the whole piece is brought inside the overall allowance. The brief’s headings stay.

Approve the save before the tool writes `.docx`.

### 9. Complete your references and citations

Harvard stays the Harvard Referencing guide. Do not restate this stage. Use `docs/referencing-harvard.md` only. A brief line about spelling every author in the text is not adopted.

### 10. Complete and submit your final draft

The tool does not submit. It prepares the final Word file and tells the student where the brief says the file goes. The final file follows the brief’s presentation. Review notes stay in their own file. The diary does not contain the marker form.

## Title page, identity, and module id

The brief’s title page wins over the usual cover. A programme name, a programme code, the sequence id, and a conclusion line appear only when this brief asks for them.

The title page carries the fields the brief lists. If the brief asks for a registration number or a student number, that number is shown. The name appears only when the brief asks for it. When the brief says the name must be left off, the name stays off the title page and off the rest of the file.

Font, spacing, margins, alignment, page numbers, and heading style follow the brief. Where the mark scheme states one of those and the brief states another, the mark scheme wins. Where the brief states them and the mark scheme is silent, the brief wins. Appendices are added when the student has them, in the place and with the labels the brief describes. This helper does not invent an appendix.

If the brief prints a module code, that code is the id, whether or not it ends in HK. If the brief prints no code, make a short id from the module title. The id is not forced to end in HK, and it is not forced to end in any other fixed letters.
