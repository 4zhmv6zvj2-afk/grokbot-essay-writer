## 2. Establish purpose and scope

The step id stays 2. Lock purpose and scope in the Business PDF’s sense: purpose and scope, stated so a section can stand alone. A report has no thesis. Do not use an engineering aim line for this lock.

- Record an initial response, possible answers, opinions, and what you already know, including related topics or frameworks, before purpose and scope are locked.
- Question the terms. Is there an agreed approach? If not, how the approaches differ, which one you will use, and how the key concepts relate. Those terms apply to purpose and scope.
- Broaden first (brainstorm, keep the ideas), then narrow. The locked purpose and scope is the narrowed claim.
- The lock is purpose and scope.

Approve the save before purpose and scope are treated as locked. `MOD1004AA` uses this label for one named supply chain.

## 3. Write a report plan

The step id stays 3. Plan the brief’s headed sections. Each section should be readable on its own. Default headings and section jobs are the Business PDF, in **Business report shape** below. Do not plan the engineering case-study body as the default.

- The plan holds the central theme and the headed sections that develop it, in the brief’s order.
- Scale the points to the report’s length and to its sections. The 3 to 4 main points guide is for an essay of about 1,000 to 1,500 words. A longer report scales its points to that length and to its sections.
- Each section has room for reasoning and gathered evidence, and names sources you expect to use.
- Revise the plan after research. If the plan is itself assessed, follow that assessment’s form.

Approve the save before the plan is locked.

## After research, revisit the plan

After the annotated bibliography and the critical review are saved, revisit the stage 3 plan before the first draft. Revisit purpose and scope only if the research breaks them. The central theme should still be the best one-sentence answer. A thin edit is allowed. A break is not a thin edit. A report has no thesis.

Check that the section arguments still hold, that the word weights still match the brief, and that each source type is a real title, with gaps named. Leave out anything the research never showed. Do not invent sources. Use only the year printed in the opened source. Do not add a second year.

If anything changes, show what changed and why, from the gathered sources, and record that in the tracking note. Approve that save again before the first draft. If purpose and scope or the central theme breaks, approve stage 2 again and then approve stage 3 again. Do not silently rewrite the plan into the draft.

## Business report shape

**Primary. Default path.** Section jobs below are from *Writing a report*. Prose and examples in this part of the file come from that PDF, not from the engineering pages.

A report communicates information. In Business School courses it also analyses and evaluates that information. Kinds named in the PDF include financial reports, case study reports (the PDF points at *Writing a case analysis*), feasibility reports, risk management reports, project reports, and academic research reports. Requirements differ by assignment.

An essay is one logical argument. The reader is the lecturer or tutor. It usually has no sub-headings, it uses transition sentences, and the reader must read the whole essay. A report uses sub-headings. Each section has a distinct purpose. A reader may open one section only. Both genres still need analysis, critical thinking, and clear precise expression.

Not every section below is in every report. University reports still show and apply the relevant theory. They are not a workplace report with the theory left out.

| Section | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| Title page | Almost always. Details vary, and a tutor may supply the page. This tool does not add a student name or number unless the brief requires one. |
| Letter of transmittal | A short cover noting terms of reference, scope, and problems addressed. Not needed for most reports. Only when the brief asks for a very formal professional model. |
| Executive summary | Purpose, findings, conclusions, and recommendations. See **Executive summary (business)**. |
| Contents | Main sections plus second and third level headings, with page numbers, when the brief asks. Not required on a short report. |
| Introduction | Brief background, purpose, scope, outline, and definitions. The purpose is explicit here even if the executive summary is skipped. |
| Body | The brief chooses the sections. Not all of these appear in every report: background, theoretical framework, review of literature, methodology, findings, discussion. At university the usual order is framework, then findings as facts, then discussion as a qualified reading of those facts. Findings are facts. Discussion is a reading of the facts. The wording shows which is which. |
| Conclusion | Always. Summarise and evaluate the findings, including the key recommendations. It may identify limitations. The language is qualified, as in the discussion. |
| Recommendations | Not always a separate section. Point or numbered form. Usually “should” statements: who should do what, where, when, how, and sometimes at what cost. |
| Appendix | Charts, tables, and other detail that would clog the body. A table or figure the argument depends on may stay in the body. |
| References | Harvard Referencing. See `docs/referencing-harvard.md`. |

Example main headings the PDF names, which are not always present: introduction, literature review, methodology, findings, discussion, conclusion.

## Executive summary (business)

**Primary default.** This is the front matter for a business report, including `MOD1004AA`.

It is a short summary for a busy reader: purpose, findings, conclusions, and recommendations. Write it after the report. Give it its own page. For an assignment, keep it to about one page. It is not needed on a short report unless the brief asks. The introduction must still make sense if this page is skipped. Some of the same material appears in both, and they are read separately, so the purpose must be clear in the introduction.

## Do not call the business summary an abstract

The PDF says the executive summary is “like an abstract or a synopsis”. That is a comparison. The heading on a business report, including `MOD1004AA`, is **Executive summary**.

## Reader and writer (business)

**Primary voice.** The reader is often an imagined client as well as the lecturer. Write clear and direct, for a busy reader. The tone is respectful (client or employer) and authoritative (the writer has the expert knowledge). University reports still show and apply the relevant theory.

Do not use I, we, or you. Keep a less familiar tone. Passive is acceptable because it can put events, ideas, and objects first. Shared academic-style habits (third person usual, no contractions, hedge, cohesion) stay in `docs/academic-writing-helpers.md`. This voice is not the engineering audience note.

## What makes a good report (business)

**Primary quality test.** From the Business PDF, not from the engineering marker list:

- The purpose is clear in the introduction.
- The introduction does not assume the executive summary was read.
- The information is sufficient and accurate.
- The methodology or theoretical framework is explained.
- The framework is used on the findings.
- The discussion uses qualifying language.
- Headings are clear and parallel, and the format fits the report type.
- The executive summary covers the report.
- Recommended actions are clear, concise, and justified from the findings.
- The language matches the writer–reader relationship.

## Engineering and science case-study report

**Structure only.** Use this subsection when the brief is an engineering or science case-study report. It is kept apart from the business table above. Do not import science or engineering voice, lab-report habits, or engineering-marker framing into the headings above.

The two pages are How to write a case study report and How to organise an engineering case study report. The writing index also names technical writing, lab reports, and a wider engineering report page. This note does not invent rules from those unread pages.

## What a case study is

**Structure only.** A case study is an activity, an event, or a problem. The writing page cites Kardos and Smith (1979) for four features of a good case. A historical case sits outside the situation. A problem-orientated case leads to recommendations, or to a new design.

## How to write it

**Structure only.** Define the task, choose the theories and analysis tools that fit, identify the problems, document the results, then recommendations and conclusions, then write. The writing page cites Jarvis (2002) on recommendations: will it work, who, when, how, and cost. Jarvis does not replace the Business PDF’s “should” recommendations on the default path.

The executive summary, introduction, conclusions, references, and appendices are usually written last.

## How to organise it

**Structure only.** Title page, executive summary, contents, introduction. The body is historical (context, approach, conclusion) or problem-orientated (facts, methodology and tools, findings, action plan). Then conclusions, appendices, and a reference list.

## Executive summary (engineering case study)

**Structure comparison only.** On the organising page the executive summary is usually read by senior management. It is an overview of the whole report. It is longer than an abstract for a professional journal. Keep it about one page and under two. It covers topic, primary aims, approach, significant findings, and recommendations. Do not retitle it Abstract. Do not replace the business one-page executive summary as the default.

## Abstract (science and engineering only)

Use an abstract only for a journal-style or postgraduate abstract. The writing index places “Writing abstracts” under postgraduate research writing, not under the case-study report. It is the shorter journal form the case-study page contrasts with the executive summary. It is not a business section, and it is not the case-study front matter.

## Where business and engineering case-study reports diverge

Voice, examples, and the default section path stay the Business PDF. The case-study pages supply a different section shape only.

- Summary length: about one page for a business assignment, under two pages on the engineering case-study page.
- Letter of transmittal: business formal reports only, and only when the brief asks.
- Introduction contents differ. The business introduction is background, purpose, scope, outline, and definitions.
- Body template: framework, then findings, then discussion on the business path; historical or problem-orientated on the case-study path.
- Recommendations on the default path use the Business “should” line, not Jarvis.
- The quality test on the default path is “What makes a good report?”, not the engineering marker list.

The brief wins if it names an analytical tool’s own structure. Do not import science or engineering voice, lab-report habits, or engineering-marker framing into `MOD1004AA`.

## Case analysis

From business-school *Writing a case analysis*. Use this when the brief is a business case. Present it as a report. This is a business source, not the engineering case-study pages.

- Name the core problem and justify that choice.
- Analyse the issues with the relevant theory and the field’s terms.
- Set out feasible alternatives and justify them with that theory. There is more than one defensible reading.
- Choose one solution and say why.
- Recommendations are precise, complementary, and feasible.

## Headings

**Primary, from the Business PDF.** Each section and subsection has a heading. Heading levels look different from each other. Leave space between sections. Headings also appear in the contents when the brief asks for a contents page.

Headings are nouns or noun phrases, parallel with each other, and not questions. The PDF’s examples of main headings include “Competitive advantages of Company X” and “Macro-economic indicators in China”. Parallel subheadings pair “Decrease in costs” with “Increase in passenger numbers”, not with “Increasing passenger numbers”.

## Marker on an engineering case study

**Structure-only context** from the organising page. It does not set this file’s quality voice. The default test is the Business PDF’s “What makes a good report?”.

The organising page says a marker looks for a clear and well-supported analysis, recommendations that can be carried out, logical organisation and cohesion, credible evidence and justification, and clear writing.
